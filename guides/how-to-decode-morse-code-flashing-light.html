How to Decode Morse Code from a Flashing Light

Whether you are tracking an emergency signal across a ridge, communicating off-grid during a blackout, or deciphering an unknown beacon, reading Morse code light pulses by eye is challenging. Here is everything you need to know about optical Morse code timing, manual decoding techniques, and automatic camera-based detection.

1. The Fundamentals of Optical Morse Timing

Standard international Morse code is defined not by absolute seconds, but by a strict relative timing unit (often called a dit duration).

International Morse Standard (ITU-R M.1677):

  • Dot (•): Light ON for 1 unit (e.g. 200 ms).
  • Dash (—): Light ON for 3 units (e.g. 600 ms).
  • Intra-character gap: Light OFF for 1 unit between dots and dashes in the same letter.
  • Letter gap: Light OFF for 3 units between different letters.
  • Word gap: Light OFF for 7 units between separate words.

2. How to Read Light Flashes by Eye

If you are in a survival situation without tools, manual decoding requires counting and tapping:

  1. Establish the baseline cadence: Watch the light cycle through 2–3 repetitions before writing anything down. Look for the longest pause (7 units) to mark the word boundaries.
  2. Count short vs. long: If a flash lasts roughly as long as a blink, record a dot (•). If it stays illuminated for three times as long, record a dash (—).
  3. Watch the pauses: A brief blink-off is between elements of the same character; a noticeable pause separates letters.
  4. Transcribe the distress pattern: The most critical optical sequence to recognize is SOS:
    • • • (pause) — — — (pause) • • •

3. Why Visual Decoding Often Fails (And How Technology Solves It)

In practice, human visual decoding suffers from three major physical limitations:

  • Human Persistence of Vision: The human retina retains light sensations for ~100 milliseconds, blurring short pulses and subtle timing gaps.
  • Hand Tremor & Distance: Staring at a distant LED across a valley or water causes visual fatigue and apparent movement (the autokinetic effect).
  • Inconsistent Sender Cadence: Unless sent by an automated beacon, human operators vary their speed. Without sub-millisecond precision, distinguishing a 400ms dash from a 250ms dot is notoriously difficult.
