Scouts & Leaders Guide

Morse Code Practice for the Signs, Signals, and Codes Merit Badge

The Signs, Signals, and Codes merit badge has a Morse code option: learn what Morse is and how it can be sent, spell your first name in it, and send or receive a short message. This page gives you everything to practice: a chart, timing, ways to send, practice messages and a troop activity. It's free to use and print.

1. What the Requirement Asks

In short, the Morse option asks you to describe Morse code and several ways to send it, spell your first name in Morse, and then send or receive a message of six to ten words. Your counselor signs it off, so check the current wording on the official merit badge page before you start. Requirements change from time to time.

2. What Morse Code Is

Morse code spells letters and numbers with two signals: a short one called a dot (•) and a long one called a dash (–). It was invented in the 1830s for the telegraph and is still used by ham radio operators, ships and pilots. Because it only needs short and long, you can send it with almost anything: light, sound, taps or radio.

The timing rule

Everything is measured in dot lengths. Get the gaps right and anyone can read you.

Part How long
Dot 1 unit
Dash 3 units
Gap inside a letter 1 unit
Gap between letters 3 units
Gap between words 7 units

Beginners often rush the gaps. A slow, even rhythm is easier to read than a fast, messy one.

4. Spell Your First Name

Find each letter of your name in the chart and write its dots and dashes, with a clear gap between letters. For example:

••• •– ––   = S A M

Then practice sending it: say it out loud ("dit dit dit, dit dah, dah dah"), tap it on a table, or flash it with a flashlight. To check your spelling, type your name into our free Morse translator, which shows the code and can flash it on your screen.

5. Ways to Send Morse Code

Anything that can be short or long can carry Morse. Here are several you can describe to your counselor:

  1. Flashlight: short flash for a dot, long flash for a dash. Works at night across a field or a lake.
  2. Signal mirror: flash sunlight toward someone far away in the daytime, covering and uncovering the mirror for dots and dashes.
  3. Sound: a whistle, a buzzer, a horn or a tone. This is how radio operators hear it.
  4. Tapping or knocking: on a table, a wall or a pipe. Use a quick tap for a dot and a double tap or a scrape for a dash.
  5. Radio: ham radio operators still send Morse (they call it CW) and can reach the other side of the world with it.
  6. Flag: one flag waved to one side for a dot and the other side for a dash.

7. Troop Activity: Lights Across the Field

A good night activity for a troop meeting or campout. It takes about 30 minutes.

  1. Split into two patrols and stand 30–50 yards apart after dark. Give each patrol a flashlight, a printed chart, paper and a pencil.
  2. Patrol A writes a secret 6–10 word message and flashes it slowly, one letter at a time. One scout reads the code aloud while another flashes.
  3. Patrol B writes down the dots and dashes as they see them, then decodes with the chart.
  4. Switch roles. The patrol with the most correct words wins.
  5. Check your answers with Morse Watcher: Point your camera at a light flashing Morse code, tap it, and Morse Watcher detects the pulses and translates them to text in real time. Compare it with what you decoded by eye.
